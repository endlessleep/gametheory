Chapter 1

Static Games with Perfect Information

Modelling simultaneous strategic interactions — and finding rational predictions for play.

Motivation

We study static games: all players choose their strategy simultaneously and independently. We assume perfect information: each player knows the complete description of the game — who is playing, what strategies are available, and what payoffs will result.

The central question: if players are rational, which strategies will they choose?

This is fundamentally harder than an individual optimisation problem. In a decision problem, one agent maximises over a known set of outcomes. In a game, each player's payoff depends on everyone else's choices — which are not yet known. The best strategy therefore depends on what you expect others to do, and they are reasoning the same way about you.

Section 1

Normal Form Games

Formal Definition
Game in Normal Form $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$

A game in normal form is defined by three objects:

$N$A finite set of players $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$.
$S_i$, for each $i$The strategy set (or action set) of player $i$ — the collection of all strategies available to her.
$g_i : \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j \to \mathbb{R}$The payoff function of player $i$ — maps every possible combination of strategies to a real number representing $i$'s outcome.

How the game is played. Each player $i$ simultaneously and independently selects a strategy $s_i \in S_i$. Once all choices are made, player $i$ receives payoff $g_i(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$.

What payoffs represent. Payoffs are real numbers encoding players' preferences over outcomes — not necessarily money. Only the ordering (ordinality) of payoffs matters: higher is better.

Notation — Read This Carefully

The notation distinguishes sharply between sets (possibilities) and elements (selections):

🧠 Memory Rule
Capital $S$ = Set
$S_i$ = all strategies available to $i$
$S = \prod_j S_j$ = all strategy profiles
$S_{-i}$ = profiles of all others
Lowercase $s$ = Selected
$s_i \in S_i$ = one specific choice by $i$
$s \in S$ = one specific profile
$s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ = everyone except $i$
$S = \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j$All possible strategy profiles (Cartesian product of all players' sets).
$s \in S$One specific strategy profile: $s = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$, one action per player.
$s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$The strategies of all players except $i$. Used to isolate player $i$'s decision problem.
$g_i(s_1, \ldots, s_N)$Payoff to player $i$ when everyone plays according to the profile $s$.
$g = (g_j)_{j=1}^{N}$The full collection of all payoff functions.
The key insight: $g_i(s_1, \ldots, s_N)$ depends on every player's choice, not just $i$'s. This is what makes it a game rather than a decision problem. Player $i$ cannot simply maximise over $s_i$ because the "objective function" $g_i(\cdot, s_{-i})$ depends on $s_{-i}$, which is chosen by others.

Matrix representation. When $N = 2$ and both $S_i$ are small and finite, the game is summarised as a matrix: Player 1 on rows, Player 2 on columns, each cell showing the payoff vector $(g_1, g_2)$.

Examples

Classic Normal Form Games

Example 1
Location Game

Two players want to choose the same location. They have no conflict of interest — they simply need to coordinate.

1 · Players
$N = 2$
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{L,\, C\}$  for each $i \in \{1,2\}$    $L$ = Location 1,  $C$ = Location 2
3 · Payoffs
$g_i(L,L) = g_i(C,C) = 1 \qquad g_i(L,C) = g_i(C,L) = 0$

Both players earn 1 when they coordinate on the same location, and 0 when they mismatch. Notice $g_1 = g_2$: the players are completely aligned.

4 · Matrix

$L$$C$
P1$L$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$

🤝 Pure Coordination
Why is this a game? Player 1's payoff depends not only on where she goes, but on where Player 2 goes. If Player 2 were to go to $L$, Player 1's best choice is $L$. If Player 2 goes to $C$, Player 1's best choice is $C$. There is no strategy that is always best.

2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{L,\, R\}$    $L$ = Left,  $R$ = Right
3 · Payoffs
$g_1(L,L) = g_1(R,R) = 0 \qquad g_1(L,R) = g_1(R,L) = 1$
$g_2(L,L) = g_2(R,R) = 1 \qquad g_2(L,R) = g_2(R,L) = 0$

PT scores when they play different sides. GK saves when they play the same side. Note: $g_1 + g_2 = 1$ always — what one wins, the other loses.

Example 3
Prisoner's Dilemma

Two suspects are held separately. Each must independently choose to cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray). Prison sentences depend on both choices. Each wants to minimise their sentence.

1 · Players
$N = 2$
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{C,\, D\}$    $C$ = Cooperate (stay silent)    $D$ = Defect (betray)
3 · Outcomes

$(C,C)$: both stay silent → each gets 3 years.

$(D,C)$: Player 1 betrays, Player 2 stays silent → Player 1 goes free (0 years), Player 2 gets 10 years.

$(C,D)$: symmetric → Player 1: 10 years, Player 2: 0 years.

$(D,D)$: both betray → each gets 5 years.

To express as a maximisation problem, payoffs = $-$years in prison.

4 · Matrix (payoffs = $-$prison years)
Player 2
$C$$D$
P1$C$$(-3,-3)$$(-10,\;0)$
$D$$(0,-10)$$(-5,-5)$
😬 Social Dilemma
The central tension. Defecting always gives a higher payoff than cooperating, regardless of what the other player does. Yet if both players follow this logic, they both get $(-5,-5)$, which is worse than the $(-3,-3)$ they would get from mutual cooperation. Individual rationality leads to a collectively suboptimal outcome.
Example 4

Two players want to spend the evening together, but disagree on the activity. Player 1 prefers Opera; Player 2 prefers Football. Being together matters to both.

Player 1 most prefers Opera together, then Football together, then being apart (0). Player 2 is the reverse. Both agree that being together is better than being apart.

Two players compete for a resource. A Hawk plays aggressively; a Dove yields peacefully.

$S_i = \{S,\, D\}$    $S$ = Safe (small prey)    $D$ = Deer (stag)

$g(S,S) = (1,1) \qquad g(D,D) = (2,2) \qquad g(S,D) = (1,0) \qquad g(D,S) = (0,1)$

The stag is worth more but requires coordination. If one player goes for the stag alone, they fail and earn 0. The safe option always yields 1, regardless of the other's choice.

Example 7
Cournot Duopoly

Two firms simultaneously choose how much to produce of a homogeneous good. The market price depends on total output, so each firm's profit is affected by the other firm's quantity.

1 · Players
$N = 2$    (Firm 1 and Firm 2)
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$    each firm $i$ chooses a quantity $q_i \geq 0$

Unlike the previous examples, there is no finite matrix here — the strategy space is continuous.

3 · Demand, Price, and Profit

Total output: $Q = q_i + q_j$, where $q_j$ is the quantity produced by the other firm.

Inverse demand: $P = \max(13 - Q,\; 0) = \max(13 - q_i - q_j,\; 0)$. The parameter $13$ characterises the demand function; the price falls as total output rises.

Cost: $c$ is the constant marginal cost of production (assumed the same for both firms).

Firm $i$'s profit:

$$g_i(q_i,\, q_j) = q_i(P - c) = q_i\bigl[\max(13 - q_i - q_j,\;0) - c\bigr]$$
Why does $q_j$ appear in firm $i$'s payoff? When firm $j$ produces more ($q_j \uparrow$), total output rises ($Q \uparrow$), the market price falls ($P \downarrow$), and firm $i$'s profit changes — even though firm $i$ did not change its own quantity. This is the strategic interdependence: what the opponent does directly affects your payoff.
No matrix representation. Since $S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$ is continuous, a matrix is impossible. The equilibrium is found analytically in Section 3.
Example 8
Second-Price Auction (Vickrey Auction)

One indivisible object is sold. Every bidder submits a bid. The highest bidder wins, but pays only the second-highest bid. Each bidder has a private valuation of the object.

1 · Players
$N \geq 2$ bidders
2 · Strategy Sets and Key Notation

$S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$: each bidder $i$ submits a bid $b_i \geq 0$.

$v_i$ = bidder $i$'s valuation (private, the maximum she is willing to pay).

$b_i$ = bidder $i$'s bid (what she actually submits; may differ from $v_i$).

$m_i(b) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$ = the highest competing bid (the highest bid among all players except $i$).

$M(b)$ = the number of bidders tied for the highest bid.

3 · Three Payoff Cases (before the formula)

Case 1 — Bidder $i$ is the unique highest bidder ($b_i > m_i(b)$): she wins and pays $m_i(b)$. Her payoff is the value she gets minus the price she pays: $g_i = v_i - m_i(b)$.

Case 2 — Bidder $i$ loses ($b_i < m_i(b)$): she does not acquire the object. Her payoff is $g_i = 0$.

Case 3 — Tie for the highest bid ($b_i = m_i(b)$): each of the $M(b)$ tied bidders wins with probability $\frac{1}{M(b)}$. The expected payoff is $g_i = \frac{v_i - m_i(b)}{M(b)}$. Note: since $b_i = m_i(b)$, the winner pays $m_i(b) = b_i$, giving expected gain $\frac{1}{M(b)}(v_i - m_i(b))$.

4 · Full Payoff Function
$$g_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - m_i(b) & \text{if } b_i > m_i(b) \\[8pt] \dfrac{v_i - m_i(b)}{M(b)} & \text{if } b_i = m_i(b) \\[8pt] 0 & \text{if } b_i < m_i(b) \end{cases}$$
Why is this a game? Bidder $i$'s payoff depends on the other bidders' bids through $m_i(b)$. If competitors bid more aggressively, $m_i(b)$ rises, and bidder $i$ either pays more (if she wins) or is more likely to lose. A key result (proved in §2.3): bidding one's true valuation $b_i = v_i$ is weakly dominant.
Classification
Families of Normal Form Games

Games can be grouped by how aligned or opposed players' interests are:

Coordination games. Players have similar interests — e.g., pure coordination (Location): payoffs are identical, $g_i = g_j$. Players want to coordinate but there is no conflict about the preferred outcome.
Conflict games. Interests are opposed. Zero-sum / constant-sum (Penalty): $g_1 = -g_2$. Hawk-Dove: best response to aggression is passivity, and vice versa.
Mixed games. Combine coordination and conflict aspects — Battle of the Sexes, Prisoner's Dilemma, Stag Hunt, Cournot.
Remark. The same game structure can represent many real-life situations. The Prisoner's Dilemma, for instance, models arms races between nations, firms deciding whether to advertise, or individuals choosing to contribute to a public good.
Section 2

Dominance in Pure Strategies

Opening Intuition

The first question is not yet: what will a rational player play?

It is simpler and more powerful: what would a rational player never play?

If one strategy is always inferior to another — no matter what opponents do — then no rational player should ever choose it. This observation allows us to eliminate bad strategies systematically and narrow down the set of plausible outcomes.

§ 2.1

Strict Dominance

Definition — Strictly Dominated Strategy

In plain words: a strategy is strictly dominated if there is always a better option, regardless of what opponents do.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominated if $\exists\, t_i \in S_i$ such that:

$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i},\quad g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i})$

We say $s_i$ is strictly dominated by $t_i$, and $t_i$ strictly dominates $s_i$.

Decoding the notation: $s_i$ is the strategy being tested. $t_i$ is the alternative. $s_{-i}$ ranges over all possible profiles the opponents could play. The condition must hold for every $s_{-i}$.

🔍 How to find in a matrix

For Player 1 (rows): compare rows using the first payoff in each cell. Strategy $s_1$ is strictly dominated by $t_1$ if, in every column, the first payoff in row $t_1$ is strictly larger than in row $s_1$.

For Player 2 (columns): compare columns using the second payoff in each cell. Strategy $s_2$ is strictly dominated by $t_2$ if, in every row, the second payoff in column $t_2$ is strictly larger than in column $s_2$.

Consequence of rationality. A rational player should never play a strictly dominated strategy: she can always do strictly better by switching to $t_i$, regardless of what others do. Rationality rules out dominated strategies.

Remark. A strictly dominated strategy may not exist. When it exists, it need not be unique — multiple strategies can be dominated.

Definition — Strictly Dominant Strategy

In plain words: a strategy is strictly dominant if it is always the best option, regardless of what opponents do.

$s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominant if for every $t_i \neq s_i$:

$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i},\quad g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$
Why only one can exist. Suppose $s_i$ and $t_i$ were both strictly dominant. Then $s_i$ strictly dominates $t_i$ (by definition of $s_i$ being dominant), and $t_i$ strictly dominates $s_i$ (by definition of $t_i$ being dominant). But these two statements directly contradict each other. So at most one strictly dominant strategy exists.

Remark. A strictly dominant strategy may not exist. When it does, it is unique.

Comparison
Dominated vs. Dominant
PropertyStrictly DominatedStrictly Dominant
What it means There exists a strategy that is strictly better for every $s_{-i}$ This strategy is strictly better than every other, for every $s_{-i}$
Rationality implication Never play it Always play it (obvious choice)
May not exist? Yes Yes
Uniqueness? Not unique — many strategies can be dominated Unique — at most one can exist
Definition — Strictly Dominant Strategy Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ is a strictly dominant strategy equilibrium if every $s_i^*$ is a strictly dominant strategy for player $i$.

Why this is a compelling prediction. Each player has an obvious, unambiguous best choice — one that is better for them no matter what others do. There is no strategic uncertainty. Remark: may not exist; when it does, it is unique.

§ 2.2

Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)

The Reasoning Process

When no player has a strictly dominant strategy, we cannot stop after one round. But we can go further using the following chain of logic:

  1. A rational player will never play a strictly dominated strategy.
  2. Every other player knows that player $i$ is rational. So they know $i$ will not play a dominated strategy.
  3. We can therefore eliminate that strategy from our model — as if it doesn't exist — and consider the smaller residual game.
  4. In the residual game, a new strategy may have become dominated (one that was not dominated before, because it was protected by the now-eliminated strategy).
  5. Repeat. This iterative reasoning is IESDS.
Formal Procedure

Let $\Gamma^1 = \Gamma$ be the original game. At each step $k \geq 1$:

  • If $\Gamma^k$ has no strictly dominated strategies: stop.
  • Otherwise: pick any player $i$ and any strictly dominated strategy $s_i \in S^k_i$. Define $\Gamma^{k+1}$ by removing $s_i$, i.e. $S^{k+1}_i = S^k_i \setminus \{s_i\}$, leaving all other strategy sets unchanged.

What $\Gamma^k$ means: $\Gamma^1$ is the full original game. $\Gamma^2$ is the game after one elimination. $\Gamma^3$ after another, and so on.

"Solvable by IESDS" means that the procedure eventually reduces every player's strategy set to a single strategy. The resulting profile is called the IESDS solution.

Two important remarks.
• The procedure may stop early, leaving a large residual game (not solvable).
• For strict dominance, the order of elimination does not matter: if the procedure produces a unique profile, that profile is always the same regardless of which dominated strategies are eliminated first.

Proposition — Uniqueness of the IESDS Solution

Suppose the IESDS procedure terminates with a unique strategy per player: $S^k_i = \{s^k_i\}$ for all $i$. Then the resulting profile $(s^k_i)_{i}$ is independent of the order of elimination. The game is said to be solvable by IESDS, and $(s^k_i)$ is its unique IESDS solution.

Additional facts:

  • The IESDS solution may not exist (the procedure may stop with many strategies remaining).
  • When it exists, it is unique.
  • IESDS rests on rationality at all orders: I am rational, I know you are rational, I know you know I am rational, etc.
§ 2.3

Weak Dominance

Motivation

Strictly dominated strategies may not exist, and IESDS may stop early — leaving many strategies and no clear prediction. We can try to relax the notion: instead of requiring a strategy to be strictly worse everywhere, allow it to be weakly worse (possibly equal somewhere, but never better).

Comparison — Strict vs. Weak Dominance
Strict DominanceWeak Dominance
Condition $g_i(t_i,s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i,s_{-i})$ for every $s_{-i}$ $g_i(t_i,s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i,s_{-i})$ for every $s_{-i}$
Ties allowed? No — must be strictly better everywhere Yes — can be equal somewhere, but never worse
Rationality argument Never play it — always has a strictly better option Possibly avoid it — you could regret it; you can never regret the dominating strategy
$$\text{Strictly dominated:} \quad \exists\, t_i:\; g_i(t_i,s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i,s_{-i}) \;\; \forall\, s_{-i}$$
$$\text{Weakly dominated:} \quad \exists\, t_i:\; g_i(t_i,s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i,s_{-i}) \;\; \forall\, s_{-i}$$
A weakly dominated strategy sometimes performs equally as well as the dominating strategy, but never better. A weakly dominant strategy performs at least as well as any other strategy, for all opponents' profiles.
Definitions — Weak Dominance

A strategy $s_i$ is weakly dominated if $\exists\, t_i$ such that $g_i(t_i,s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i,s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$.

A strategy $s_i$ is weakly dominant if for all $t_i$: $g_i(s_i,s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i,s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$.

A strategy profile $s^*$ is a dominant strategy equilibrium if every $s_i^*$ is weakly dominant.

Example — Motivation for Weak Dominance
Are there strictly dominant strategies? What is a smart choice?
$L$$R$
P1$U$$(2,2)$$(4,4)$
$D$$(3,1)$$(4,1)$
1
Strictly dominant? Does $D$ strictly dominate $U$? Against $L$: $g_1(D,L)=3>g_1(U,L)=2$ ✓. Against $R$: $g_1(D,R)=4=g_1(U,R)=4$ — equality, not strict. So $D$ does not strictly dominate $U$. No strictly dominant strategy.
2
Weakly dominant? Does $D$ weakly dominate $U$? Against $L$: $3 \geq 2$ ✓. Against $R$: $4 \geq 4$ ✓. $D$ weakly dominates $U$ for Player 1. Playing $D$ is "smart": you can never regret it (you never do worse), and sometimes you do strictly better (when P2 plays $L$).
Exercise — Iterated Elimination of Weakly Dominated Strategies
$L$$R$
P1$U$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$
$M$$(1,1)$$(2,1)$
$D$$(0,0)$$(2,1)$
1
$M$ weakly dominates $U$? Against $L$: $g_1(M,L)=1=g_1(U,L)$ ✓ (equal). Against $R$: $g_1(M,R)=2>g_1(U,R)=0$ ✓. Yes — eliminate $U$.
2
After eliminating $U$: $\{M,D\}\times\{L,R\}$. For P2: $g_2(M,L)=1$ and $g_2(M,R)=1$; $g_2(D,L)=0$ and $g_2(D,R)=1$. Does $R$ weakly dominate $L$? Against $M$: $1=1$ ✓; Against $D$: $1>0$ ✓. Eliminate $L$.
3
After eliminating $L$: $\{M,D\}\times\{R\}$. In column $R$: $g_1(M,R)=2=g_1(D,R)=2$. Neither $M$ nor $D$ dominates the other. Procedure stops with two strategies remaining.
⚠️
Key point — Order dependence. If we had first eliminated $D$ instead of $U$, we might reach a different residual game. This is a fundamental limitation of weak dominance: different elimination orders can produce different results. The method is inconclusive in general — it predicts a set of possible outcomes, not a unique one. This motivates the need for Nash equilibrium.
Exercise
Second-Price Auction: Prove that bidding your true value is weakly dominant

Prove that in a second-price auction, playing $b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy for every bidder $i$.

1
Strategy. Fix bidder $i$ with valuation $v_i$. Fix any bid profile $b_{-i}$ of the opponents and let $m = m_i(b_{-i})$ be the highest competing bid. We compare $b_i = v_i$ against any alternative bid $b_i' \neq v_i$.
2
Case 1: $v_i > m$ (truthful bid wins).
With $b_i = v_i$: wins, pays $m$, earns $v_i - m > 0$.
• $b_i' > m$: also wins, earns $v_i - m$ → same payoff.
• $b_i' \leq m$: loses, earns $0 < v_i - m$ → worse.
Truthful bidding is at least as good.
3
Case 2: $v_i < m$ (truthful bid loses).
With $b_i = v_i$: loses, earns $0$.
• $b_i' < m$: also loses, earns $0$ → same payoff.
• $b_i' > m$: wins, pays $m > v_i$, earns $v_i - m < 0$ → worse.
Truthful bidding is at least as good.
4
Case 3: $v_i = m$ (tie).
With $b_i = v_i = m$: tied, earns $\frac{v_i-m}{M(b)} = 0$.
Any deviation either stays at 0 (if $b_i' = m$) or risks a loss (if $b_i' > m$ and wins while $v_i = m$). Truthful bidding is at least as good.
✓
Conclusion. In all cases, $g_i(b_i=v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$ for every $b_i'$ and every $b_{-i}$. Therefore $b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy. $\blacksquare$
Section 3

Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

§ 3.1

Definition and Best Response

Intuition First

IESDS gives us a prediction when it fully solves the game, but often it does not. We need a more general concept.

Nash equilibrium answers the question: which strategy profiles are self-sustaining?

A strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium if no player wants to change their own strategy, given what the others are doing. The key word is alone: we only consider unilateral deviations. A player considers switching while assuming all others stay put. If no single player can benefit from such a switch, the profile is stable — a Nash equilibrium.

Intuitively: at a Nash equilibrium, every player is making the best possible decision given the decisions of everyone else. Nobody regrets their choice.

Formal Definition — Nash Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_N) \in S$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i$ and every strategy $t_i \in S_i$:

$$g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$$

Decoding the notation:

  • $s_{-i}$: the strategies of all players except $i$ — these are held fixed in the definition.
  • $t_i$: any alternative strategy that player $i$ could switch to — this is the potential deviation.
  • The condition says: given $s_{-i}$, player $i$ cannot do strictly better than $s_i$ by switching unilaterally.
Profitable deviation. If $\exists\, t_i$ such that $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$, then $t_i$ is a profitable unilateral deviation for player $i$. A Nash equilibrium has no profitable unilateral deviations for any player.

Notation: $NE(\Gamma)$ = set of Nash equilibria of $\Gamma$. Remark: a NE may not exist, and may not be unique.

Definition — Best Response

In plain words: a best response is the best strategy available to player $i$, given what all others are doing.

Formally: $s_i \in S_i$ is a best response to $s_{-i}$ if for every $t_i \in S_i$: $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$.

The set of all best responses to $s_{-i}$ is denoted $BR_i(s_{-i})$. Note: $BR_i$ can contain more than one strategy when two strategies yield the same maximal payoff — there can be ties.

$$s \text{ is a Nash equilibrium} \iff s_i \in BR_i(s_{-i}) \text{ for every player } i$$
A Nash equilibrium is precisely a strategy profile where every player is simultaneously best-responding to all others. It is a mutual best response.
🔍 Practical Method — Finding Nash Equilibria in a Matrix

Step 1 — Player 1's best responses: For each column (i.e., for each strategy of P2), look at the first payoff in each cell of that column. Underline the maximum value(s). These are P1's best responses to that column.

Step 2 — Player 2's best responses: For each row (i.e., for each strategy of P1), look at the second payoff in each cell of that row. Underline the maximum value(s). These are P2's best responses to that row.

Step 3 — Nash equilibria: A cell $(s_1, s_2)$ is a Nash equilibrium if and only if it has both payoffs underlined — meaning both players are simultaneously best-responding.

Five Interpretations of Nash Equilibrium
Prescription. An external authority suggests $s$. No player has an incentive to deviate → the recommendation is self-enforcing.
Agreement. Players communicate and agree on $s$ in advance. Nash equilibria are the only agreements that no one will break unilaterally.
Introspection. A player reasons through the game and concludes $s$ is the only consistent outcome. (Applies most cleanly when the NE is unique.)
Convention / social norm. Social rules (e.g., driving on the right) are Nash equilibria: everyone follows them because everyone else does.
Learning or evolution. Through repeated interaction, players may converge to a profile where no one benefits from switching — a Nash equilibrium.

Exercise — Cournot Duopoly
Find the Nash equilibrium. Use two methods: (i) best responses, (ii) no profitable deviation. Sketch the best response functions.
1
Setup. $P = 13 - q_i - q_j$ (assume $q_1+q_2 \leq 13$), $c=0$ for simplicity. Firm $i$'s profit: $g_i = q_i(13-q_i-q_j)$.
2
Method 1 — Best responses. Firm $i$ maximises over $q_i$ taking $q_j$ as given. First-order condition:
$$\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q_i} = 13 - 2q_i - q_j = 0 \implies BR_i(q_j) = \frac{13-q_j}{2}$$
This reaction function is downward-sloping: the more $j$ produces, the less $i$ wants to produce.
3
Equilibrium. Solve both reaction functions simultaneously:
$q_1^* = \frac{13-q_2^*}{2}$ and $q_2^* = \frac{13-q_1^*}{2}$. By symmetry: $q_1^*=q_2^*=q^*$. Then $q^* = \frac{13-q^*}{2} \implies 2q^* = 13-q^* \implies q^* = \frac{13}{3}$.
✓
Nash equilibrium: $q_1^*=q_2^*=\frac{13}{3}$. Price: $P^*=13-\frac{26}{3}=\frac{13}{3}$. Each firm's profit: $\frac{13}{3}\cdot\frac{13}{3}=\frac{169}{9}\approx18.8$.

Critical distinction: Strict IESDS preserves all Nash equilibria ($NE(\Gamma^{k+1})=NE(\Gamma^k)$). Iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies can destroy some Nash equilibria ($NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$, not necessarily equal).
Proposition 1 — Strict Dominance and Nash Equilibria

(1) An equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies is the unique Nash equilibrium.

(2) Let $(\Gamma^k)$ be the IESDS sequence. For every $k\geq 1$: $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$. (IESDS never eliminates Nash equilibria.)

(3) If $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS, the resulting profile is the unique Nash equilibrium.

1
Proof of (1) — existence. Let $s^*$ be the strictly dominant strategy equilibrium. For every $i$ and $t_i \neq s_i^*$: $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$, hence in particular for $s_{-i}^*$. So $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $t_i$: $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium.
2
Proof of (1) — uniqueness. Suppose $s' \neq s^*$ is also a NE. Then $\exists\, i$ with $s_i' \neq s_i^*$. Since $s_i^*$ strictly dominates $s_i'$: $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}') > g_i(s_i', s_{-i}')$. But this contradicts $s'$ being a NE (which requires $s_i'$ to be a best response to $s_{-i}'$). $\blacksquare$
3
Proof of (3). By (2), $NE(\Gamma^k)=NE(\Gamma)$ for all $k$. Since $\Gamma^k$ has only one strategy per player (say $\bar{s}$), $NE(\Gamma^k)=\{\bar{s}\}$. Hence $NE(\Gamma)=\{\bar{s}\}$. $\blacksquare$
Proposition 2 — Weak Dominance and Nash Equilibria

(1) An equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium.

(2) Iterating elimination of weakly dominated strategies: $NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$. The inclusion may be strict — weakly dominated strategies can be Nash equilibria, and eliminating them removes those equilibria.

1
Proof. Let $s^*$ be a dominant strategy equilibrium. For every $i$ and $t_i$: $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$. In particular at $s_{-i}=s_{-i}^*$: $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $t_i$. This is exactly the Nash equilibrium condition. $\blacksquare$

How to Analyse a Static Normal-Form Game

Step-by-Step Algorithm
1
Look for strictly dominant strategies.

For each player, check whether one strategy yields strictly higher payoffs than all others, for every possible opponents' profile.
If every player has one → the profile is the unique dominant strategy equilibrium and the unique Nash equilibrium. Stop.

2
Run IESDS (iterative elimination of strictly dominated strategies).

Iteratively remove strategies that are strictly dominated in the current residual game. Keep going until no more eliminations are possible.
If exactly one strategy per player survives → this is the unique Nash equilibrium. Stop.
Remember: IESDS never eliminates Nash equilibria.

3
Find Nash equilibria directly using best responses.

Method A — Best responses: for each column mark P1's best first payoff(s); for each row mark P2's best second payoff(s). Cells with both marks are Nash equilibria.
Method B — No profitable deviation: for each cell, check whether any player can strictly improve by switching unilaterally. Cells where no one can improve are Nash equilibria.
