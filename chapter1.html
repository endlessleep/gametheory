Chapter 1

Static Games with Perfect Information

Modelling simultaneous strategic interactions where all players know the full description of the game — and finding rational predictions for play.

Motivation

In this chapter, we study static games: all players choose their strategy simultaneously, without observing the others' choices before acting. We further assume perfect information: every player knows the full description of the game — the set of players, all available strategies, and all payoff functions.

The central question is: if players are rational, which strategies are they most likely to choose? This is fundamentally different from a single-agent optimisation problem, because a player's payoff depends not only on her own action, but also on the actions of all other players. A player's best strategy therefore depends on what she anticipates others will play.

Section 1

Normal Form Games

Definition
Game in Normal Form

A game in normal form $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$ is defined by three objects:

$N$ A finite set of players $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$
$S_i$ for each $i$ A set of pure strategies (or actions) available to player $i$
$g_i : \prod_{j=1}^N S_j \to \mathbb{R}$ A payoff function for each player $i$, mapping every strategy profile to a real number
Interpretation. Each player $i$ simultaneously and independently selects a strategy $s_i \in S_i$. Once all choices are made, player $i$ receives payoff $g_i(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$.
Notation
Standard Notation for Normal Form Games
$S = \prod_{j=1}^N S_j$Joint strategy set (all players' strategies combined)
$s_i \in S_i$Individual action of player $i$
$s \in S$Strategy profile (one action per player)
$s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$Strategy profile of all players except player $i$
$g = (g_j)_{j \in \{1,\ldots,N\}}$Collection of all players' payoff functions
Important remark. Payoffs are real numbers representing players' preferences over outcomes — not necessarily monetary rewards. Only the ordinality of payoffs matters: how different outcomes compare to one another.

When $N = 2$ and both strategy sets are finite and small, the game can be conveniently represented as a matrix. By convention, the row player is Player 1, the column player is Player 2, and each cell contains the vector of payoffs $(g_1, g_2)$.

2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{L,\, C\} \quad \text{for each } i \in \{1,2\}$

3 · Payoffs
$g_i(L,L) = g_i(C,C) = 1 \qquad g_i(L,C) = g_i(C,L) = 0$

Both players receive payoff $1$ when they coordinate on the same location, and $0$ otherwise. Payoffs are identical for both players.

🤝 Pure Coordination

⚽ Example 2
Penalty Kick

A penalty taker (PT) chooses where to shoot; a goalkeeper (GK) chooses where to dive. The penalty taker scores if they pick different sides; the goalkeeper saves if they pick the same side.

1 · Players
$N = 2 \quad$ (Player 1 = Penalty Taker, Player 2 = Goalkeeper)
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{L,\, R\} \quad$ where $L$ = Left, $R$ = Right
3 · Payoffs
$g_1(L,L) = g_1(R,R) = 0 \qquad g_1(L,R) = g_1(R,L) = 1$
$g_2(L,L) = g_2(R,R) = 1 \qquad g_2(L,R) = g_2(R,L) = 0$

Note that $g_1 + g_2 = 1$ always: the game is constant-sum, hence strategically equivalent to a zero-sum game.

4 · Matrix
Goalkeeper
$L$ $R$
Penalty Taker $L$ $(0,1)$ $(1,0)$
$R$ $(1,0)$ $(0,1)$

No pure strategy Nash equilibrium exists here — see Section 3.

⚔️ Conflict / Constant-Sum
The players have completely opposing interests. Whatever is good for one is bad for the other. This is strategically equivalent to a zero-sum game.
🔒 Example 3
Prisoner's Dilemma

Two suspects are held separately and must each decide independently whether to cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray the other). Each player wants to minimise their prison time.

1 · Players
$N = 2$
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{C,\, D\} \quad$ where $C$ = Cooperate (stay silent), $D$ = Defect (betray)
3 · Outcomes

Both cooperate ($C,C$): each receives 3 years. One defects while the other cooperates: the defector goes free (0 years), the cooperator receives 10 years. Both defect ($D,D$): each receives 5 years.

To express as a maximisation problem, we negate prison years.

4 · Matrix (payoffs = $-$years in prison)
Player 2

Player 1 $C$ $(-3,-3)$ $(-10,\;0)$
$D$ $(0,-10)$ $(-5,-5)$
😬 Social Dilemma
Key insight. Defection $D$ is individually rational for each player regardless of what the other does. Yet mutual defection $(-5,-5)$ is collectively worse than mutual cooperation $(-3,-3)$. This tension between individual and collective rationality is the hallmark of the Prisoner's Dilemma.

🦅 Example 5
Hawk-Dove

Two players compete for a resource worth $2$. A Hawk plays aggressively; a Dove yields. If both play Hawk, they fight and each suffers a cost of $2$.

1 · Players
$N = 2$
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{H,\, D\} \quad$ where $H$ = Hawk, $D$ = Dove
3 · Payoffs
$g(H,H) = (-1,-1) \qquad g(H,D) = (2,0) \qquad g(D,H) = (0,2) \qquad g(D,D) = (1,1)$

🔄 Anti-Coordination
Anti-coordination structure. The best response to $H$ is $D$, and vice versa. Players want to play different strategies. Widely used in evolutionary biology and economics to model resource competition.
🦌 Example 6
Stag Hunt

Two hunters can each pursue a small, safe prey alone (the "safe" option), or cooperate to hunt a stag (the "cooperative" option). Hunting the stag requires both players to choose it.

1 · Players
$N = 2$
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \{S,\, D\} \quad$ where $S$ = Safe (small prey), $D$ = Deer (stag)
3 · Payoffs
$g(S,S) = (1,1) \qquad g(D,D) = (2,2) \qquad g(S,D) = (1,0) \qquad g(D,S) = (0,1)$
4 · Matrix
Player 2
$S$ $D$
Player 1 $S$ $(1,1)$ $(1,0)$
$D$ $(0,1)$ $(2,2)$
🤝 Coordination
Risk vs. reward. $(D,D)$ is Pareto superior — it yields the best collective outcome — but it requires trust. If a player suspects the other may not cooperate, deviating to $S$ is safer. $(S,S)$ is a Nash equilibrium too, but it is Pareto dominated by $(D,D)$.
🏭 Example 7
Cournot Duopoly

Two firms simultaneously choose how much to produce of a homogeneous good. The market price is determined by total output. This is the classic model of quantity competition.

1 · Players
$N = 2 \quad$ (Firm 1 and Firm 2)
2 · Strategy Sets
$S_i = \mathbb{R}_+ \quad$ each firm $i$ chooses a quantity $q_i \geq 0$

Total output: $Q = q_1 + q_2$. Inverse demand: $P = \max(13 - Q,\; 0)$.

3 · Payoffs — Profit Functions
$g_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i \cdot (P - c) = q_i \cdot \bigl[\max(13 - q_i - q_j,\; 0) - c\bigr]$
where $c \geq 0$ is the constant marginal cost of production. Firm $i$'s profit depends on its own quantity $q_i$ and on the rival's quantity $q_j$.
No matrix representation. Since $S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$ is continuous, the game cannot be summarised in a finite matrix. Instead, the best responses are derived by first-order conditions. The Nash equilibrium will be found analytically in Section 3.
🔨 Example 8
Second-Price Auction (Vickrey Auction)

One indivisible object is sold to $N \geq 2$ bidders. The auction rule: the highest bidder wins, but pays the second-highest bid.

1 · Players
$N \in \mathbb{N},\; N \geq 2$
2 · Strategy Sets & Notation
$S_i = \mathbb{R}_+ \quad$ each bidder $i$ submits a bid $b_i \geq 0$

Each bidder $i$ has a private valuation $v_i$ (the maximum they are willing to pay). Define $m_i(b) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$ as the highest competing bid.

3 · Payoff Function
$$g_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - m_i(b) & \text{if } b_i > m_i(b) \quad \text{(unique winner)} \\[8pt] \dfrac{v_i - m_i(b)}{M(b)} & \text{if } b_i = m_i(b) \quad \text{(tie: } M(b) \text{ bidders share)} \\[8pt] 0 & \text{if } b_i < m_i(b) \quad \text{(loser)} \end{cases}$$

$M(b)$ denotes the number of bidders tied for the highest bid. Each tied bidder wins with equal probability $\frac{1}{M(b)}$.

Key result (to be proved in Section 2.3): bidding one's true valuation $b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy in a second-price auction. This remarkable property makes the Vickrey auction incentive-compatible.
Classification
Families of Games

Games can be grouped into broad families based on the alignment or opposition of players' interests:

Coordination Games — players have similar interests and benefit from choosing the same (or compatible) strategies.
Pure coordination (e.g., Location): payoffs are identical for all players, $g_i = g_j$. Players simply need to coordinate on any equilibrium.
Conflict Games — players' interests are completely opposed.
Zero-sum / Constant-sum (e.g., Penalty): $g_1 = -g_2$. Any gain for one player is an equal loss for the other.
Hawk-Dove: the best response to an aggressive strategy is a passive one, and vice versa (anti-coordination).
Mixed Games — combine elements of coordination and conflict.
(e.g., Battle of the Sexes, Prisoner's Dilemma, Stag Hunt). Players agree on some aspects but disagree on others.
Remark. A given game structure can represent many real-life situations. For instance, the Prisoner's Dilemma models: arms races between countries, firms' decisions on whether to advertise, or individuals' choices about contributing to a public good.
Section 2

Dominance in Pure Strategies

Motivation

To understand what rational players will play, we first ask the reverse question: what would a rational player never play? If a strategy is always inferior to another — regardless of opponents' choices — then no rational player should choose it. This is the idea of dominance.

§ 2.1

Strictly Dominated Strategies

Definition — Strictly Dominated Strategy

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominated if there exists $t_i \in S_i$ such that:

$\forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i},\quad g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i})$

We say $s_i$ is strictly dominated by $t_i$, or that $t_i$ strictly dominates $s_i$.

Interpretation. A strictly dominated strategy yields a strictly lower payoff than some alternative strategy $t_i$, for every possible action profile of the opponents. A rational player should never play a strictly dominated strategy: they can always do strictly better by switching to $t_i$, no matter what others do.

Remark. A strictly dominated strategy may not exist. When it exists, it need not be unique.

Definition — Strictly Dominant Strategy

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominant if it strictly dominates every other strategy. That is, for any $t_i \in S_i$ with $t_i \neq s_i$:

$\forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i},\quad g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$
Interpretation. A strictly dominant strategy yields a strictly better payoff than any alternative, regardless of what others play. If a player has such a strategy, it is an obvious and unambiguous choice.

Remark. A strictly dominant strategy may not exist. When it does exist, it is unique.

Definition — Strictly Dominant Strategy Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_N) \in S$ is a strictly dominant strategy equilibrium if for every player $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$, $s_i$ is strictly dominant.

If such an equilibrium exists, it is the unique rational outcome of the game. Remark. A strictly dominant strategy equilibrium need not exist. When it does, it is unique.

$C$$D$
P1 $C$$(-3,-3)$$(-10,\;0)$
$D$$(0,-10)$$(-5,-5)$

§ 2.2

Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)

Motivation

When no player has a strictly dominant strategy, we cannot immediately predict the outcome. However, we can go further: if a rational player would never choose a strictly dominated strategy, then the other players know this. They can eliminate that strategy from their reasoning and update their own best response accordingly. This iterative logic is the basis of IESDS.

🔄 Example — Introductory IESDS

What should Player 1 play if she knows Player 2 is rational?

$S$$T$
P1 $S$$(0,-2)$$(-10,-1)$
$T$$(-1,-10)$$(-5,-5)$
1
Player 2's perspective — Does $T$ strictly dominate $S$?
If P1 plays $S$: $g_2(S,T) = -1 > g_2(S,S) = -2$ ✓
If P1 plays $T$: $g_2(T,T) = -5 > g_2(T,S) = -10$ ✓
→ $T$ strictly dominates $S$ for Player 2. A rational Player 2 will never play $S$.
2
Player 1 knows Player 2 is rational, so Player 1 eliminates $S$ from Player 2's strategy set. The reduced game has only column $T$.
3
Player 1 in the reduced game:
$g_1(S,T) = -10 < g_1(T,T) = -5$
→ Player 1 plays $T$.
4
IESDS solution: $(T,T)$.

Formal Procedure
Iterative Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies

The IESDS procedure is defined iteratively. Let $\Gamma^1 = \Gamma$. At each step $k \geq 1$:

  • If $\Gamma^k$ has no strictly dominated strategies, the procedure stops.
  • Otherwise, pick a player $i$ and a strictly dominated strategy $s_i \in S^k_i$. Define $\Gamma^{k+1}$ by removing $s_i$ from $S^k_i$: $S^{k+1}_i = S^k_i \setminus \{s_i\}$, keeping all other strategy sets unchanged.
Remarks.
• In general, the procedure is not unique: its result may depend on the order in which strategies are eliminated.
• It may terminate early (leaving a large residual game) or may run until only one profile remains.
Proposition — Uniqueness when Solvable

Suppose there exists $k \geq 1$ such that for all $i$, $S^k_i$ is a singleton $\{s^k_i\}$. Then the strategy profile $(s^k_i)_{i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}}$ does not depend on the order of elimination. We say the game is solvable by IESDS, and $(s^k_i)$ is its unique solution.

Remarks.
• The IESDS solution may not exist (the procedure may not fully reduce the game).
• When it exists, it is unique.
• If an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies exists, the game is solvable by IESDS and the two solutions coincide.
• IESDS relies on rationality at all orders: players are rational, they know others are rational, they know others know they are rational, and so on.

§ 2.3

Weak Dominance

Motivation

Strictly dominated strategies may not exist, in which case IESDS yields no clear prediction. We can try to relax the notion: instead of requiring a strategy to be strictly worse, we allow it to be at least as bad (weakly worse).

🔍 Example — Motivating Weak Dominance

Are there strictly dominant strategies? What would be a smart choice?

$L$$R$
P1 $U$$(2,2)$$(4,4)$
$D$$(3,1)$$(4,1)$
1
Strictly dominant strategies for P1?
$g_1(U,L)=2 < g_1(D,L)=3$ → col $L$: $D$ better.
$g_1(U,R)=4 = g_1(D,R)=4$ → col $R$: tied.
$D$ does not strictly dominate $U$ (requires strict inequality everywhere). No strictly dominant strategy.
2
Weak dominance? $D$ weakly dominates $U$: $g_1(D,s_{-1}) \geq g_1(U,s_{-1})$ for all $s_{-1}$, with strict inequality when P2 plays $L$. Playing $D$ is a "smart" choice: you can never regret it.
Definition — Weakly Dominated Strategy

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is (weakly) dominated if there exists $t_i \in S_i$ such that:

$\forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i},\quad g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i})$

We say $s_i$ is dominated by $t_i$. Note: this requires only $\geq$, not strict $>$. A dominated strategy is not necessarily a terrible choice, but one can potentially regret it.

Definition — Weakly Dominant Strategy & Equilibrium

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is dominant if it weakly dominates every other strategy:

$\forall\, t_i \in S_i,\; \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i},\quad g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$

A strategy profile $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_N)$ is a dominant strategy equilibrium if every $s_i$ is dominant for player $i$.

A dominant strategy is a "reasonable" choice: playing it guarantees that a player can never regret their decision in hindsight. A dominant strategy equilibrium — when it exists — seems compelling, since no player ever benefits from deviating.

$M$ weakly dominates $U$: $g_1(M,L)=1=g_1(U,L)$ and $g_1(M,R)=2>g_1(U,R)=0$. ✓ Eliminate $U$.
Reduced game $\{M,D\} \times \{L,R\}$: then $L$ weakly dominated by $R$ for P2. Eliminate $L$.
Reduced $\{M,D\} \times \{R\}$: $g_1(M,R)=2 > g_1(D,R)=2$? No — both equal 2. Eliminate $D$ (dominated by $M$, since $g_1(M,L)=1>g_1(D,L)=0$ — but $L$ was already eliminated). Multiple equilibria remain.
2
Remark — Order Dependence. Different elimination orders lead to different residual games and potentially different "solutions." This is a key limitation of weak dominance compared to strict dominance.
3
Conclusion. The IEWDS procedure is inconclusive: it does not predict a unique strategy profile, only a set of candidate outcomes. This motivates the need for a sharper solution concept — Nash Equilibrium.

Second-Price Auction: Truthful Bidding is Weakly Dominant

Prove that in a second-price auction, bidding one's true valuation $b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy for each bidder $i$.

1
Setup. Fix any bidder $i$ with valuation $v_i$. Fix any bid profile $b_{-i}$ of the other bidders, and let $m = m_i(b_{-i}) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$ be the highest competing bid. We compare bidding $b_i = v_i$ against any other bid $b_i' \neq v_i$.
2
Case A: $v_i > m$ (truthful bid wins).
With $b_i = v_i$: bidder $i$ wins and receives $v_i - m > 0$.
• If $b_i' > m$: still wins, receives $v_i - m$ → same payoff.
• If $b_i' \leq m$: loses, receives $0 < v_i - m$ → worse.
3
Case B: $v_i < m$ (truthful bid loses).
With $b_i = v_i$: bidder $i$ loses, receives $0$.
• If $b_i' < m$: still loses, receives $0$ → same payoff.
• If $b_i' > m$: wins but pays $m > v_i$, receives $v_i - m < 0$ → worse.
4
Case C: $v_i = m$.
With $b_i = v_i = m$: tied, receives $0$ (since $v_i - m = 0$).
Any deviation either keeps payoff at $0$ or makes it negative. → Same or worse.
5

Section 3

Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

§ 3.1

Definition and Best Responses

Motivation

IESDS may not fully reduce a game, leaving many candidate outcomes. We need a more general solution concept. The most important one in all of game theory is the Nash equilibrium: a strategy profile in which no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally.

Definition — Nash Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N) \in S$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$ and every strategy $t_i \in S_i$:

$g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$
Interpretation. At a Nash equilibrium, no player can profitably deviate — no player can strictly increase their payoff by unilaterally changing their own strategy, given that all other players stick to $s_{-i}$. We fix everyone else's strategy and check whether player $i$ wants to switch: if no one does, the profile is a Nash equilibrium.

Remark. A Nash equilibrium may not exist, and may not be unique. We denote by $NE(\Gamma)$ the set of Nash equilibria, and by $E(\Gamma) = \{(g_1(s), \ldots, g_N(s)) : s \in NE(\Gamma)\} \subset \mathbb{R}^N$ the set of Nash equilibrium payoff vectors.

Definition — Best Response

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is a best response of player $i$ to a profile $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ if, for every $t_i \in S_i$:

$g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$

The set of best responses to $s_{-i}$ is denoted $BR_i(s_{-i})$.

Proposition — Nash Equilibrium via Best Responses

A strategy profile $s \in S$ is a Nash equilibrium if and only if for every player $i$:

$s_i \in BR_i(s_{-i})$

In other words, a Nash equilibrium is a mutual best response: each player is simultaneously best-responding to all other players' strategies.

Interpretations of Nash Equilibrium
Prescription: An external authority proposes $s$. No player wants to deviate → the recommendation is self-enforcing.
Agreement: Players pre-communicate and agree to play $s$. Nash equilibria are the only agreements that no one will break.
Introspection: A player reasons about the game and concludes that $s$ is the only consistent outcome. (Applicable only when the Nash equilibrium is unique.)
Convention: Social norms (e.g., driving on the right) are Nash equilibria — everyone follows them because everyone else does.
Learning / Evolution: If players repeatedly interact and adapt, they may converge to a profile where no one benefits from switching — a Nash equilibrium.

§ 3.2

Nash Equilibria, Dominant and Dominated Strategies

Proposition — Links between Solution Concepts (1)

1. An equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.

2. Let $(\Gamma^k)$ be the sequence of games obtained by IESDS. Then for every $k \geq 1$:

$NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$

All Nash equilibria survive the IESDS procedure — eliminating strictly dominated strategies does not remove any Nash equilibrium.

3. If $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS, then the strategy profile obtained is the unique Nash equilibrium.

1
Proof of (1). Let $s^*$ be the strictly dominant strategy equilibrium. For every $i$ and every $t_i \neq s^*_i$, $g_i(s^*_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$, hence in particular for $s_{-i} = s^*_{-i}$. Thus $s^*_i$ is a strict best response to $s^*_{-i}$ for every $i$: $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium. Uniqueness: suppose $s' \neq s^*$ is also a Nash equilibrium. Then there exists $i$ with $s'_i \neq s^*_i$. Since $s^*_i$ strictly dominates $s'_i$, $g_i(s^*_i, s'_{-i}) > g_i(s'_i, s'_{-i})$, contradicting $s'$ being a Nash equilibrium. $\blacksquare$
2
Proof of (3). Let $\bar{s}$ be the unique strategy profile surviving IESDS. By statement (2), $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$ for all $k$. Since $\Gamma^k$ has a unique strategy profile $\bar{s}$, the only Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma^k$ is $\bar{s}$ (the game is trivial). Hence $NE(\Gamma) = \{\bar{s}\}$. $\blacksquare$
Proposition — Links between Solution Concepts (2)

1. An equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium.

2. Let $(\Gamma^k)$ be the sequence obtained by iterative elimination of weakly dominated strategies. Then for every $k \geq 1$:

$NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \;\subseteq\; NE(\Gamma^k)$

Eliminating weakly dominated strategies may lose some Nash equilibria — unlike strict dominance, this inclusion may be strict.

1
Proof of (1). Let $s^* = (s^*_i)$ be a dominant strategy equilibrium. For every $i$, $s^*_i$ is dominant, meaning $g_i(s^*_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $t_i$ and all $s_{-i}$. In particular, taking $s_{-i} = s^*_{-i}$, we get $g_i(s^*_i, s^*_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s^*_{-i})$ for all $t_i$. This is exactly the Nash equilibrium condition. Hence $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium. $\blacksquare$

Find the dominated strategies and the Nash equilibria of the following game.

$A$$B$
P1 $A$$(2,2)$$(1,2)$
$B$$(2,1)$$(1,1)$
1
Dominated strategies for P1:
$A$ vs $B$: $g_1(A,A)=2=g_1(B,A)=2$ and $g_1(A,B)=1=g_1(B,B)=1$. Payoffs are identical → neither strategy dominates the other. No dominated strategy for P1.
2
Dominated strategies for P2:
$g_2(\cdot,A)=(2,1)$ vs $g_2(\cdot,B)=(2,1)$. Payoffs are identical in each row → no dominated strategy for P2 either.
3

P1: $g_1(A,A)=2=g_1(B,A)$ → $BR_1(A)=\{A,B\}$. $g_1(A,B)=1=g_1(B,B)$ → $BR_1(B)=\{A,B\}$.
P2: $g_2(A,A)=2>g_2(A,B)=2$? Tie. $BR_2(A)=\{A,B\}$. $g_2(B,A)=1=g_2(B,B)=1$ → $BR_2(B)=\{A,B\}$.
Every profile is a mutual best response: $NE = \{(A,A),(A,B),(B,A),(B,B)\}$.
Summary — Step-by-Step Method for Static Games
How to Analyse Any Normal Form Game
1
Search for strictly dominant strategies.
If a profile of strictly dominant strategies exists — stop. This is the unique rational prediction.
2
Run IESDS.
Iteratively eliminate strictly dominated strategies. If the game reduces to a single profile — stop. That profile is the unique Nash equilibrium.
3
Find Nash equilibria directly.
Use one of the two equivalent approaches:
• No profitable deviation: check each profile and verify no player can strictly improve by switching unilaterally.
• Best responses: compute $BR_i(s_{-i})$ for each player and find profiles where all players are simultaneously best-responding.

Cournot Duopoly — Find Nash Equilibrium

Find the Nash equilibria of the Cournot game (Example 7) using both methods: (i) no profitable deviation, and (ii) best responses. Plot the best response functions on a graph.

1
Setup. $P = 13 - q_1 - q_2$ (assuming $q_1 + q_2 \leq 13$), $c = 0$ for simplicity. Profit of firm $i$: $g_i = q_i(13 - q_i - q_j)$.
2
Best response of firm $i$. Maximise over $q_i$, taking $q_j$ as given: $$\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q_i} = 13 - 2q_i - q_j = 0 \implies q_i^* = \frac{13 - q_j}{2}$$ This is the best response function (reaction function) $BR_i(q_j)$.
3
Nash equilibrium. Solve the system of simultaneous best responses: $$q_1 = \frac{13 - q_2}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad q_2 = \frac{13 - q_1}{2}$$ By symmetry $q_1^* = q_2^*$: $q^* = \frac{13 - q^*}{2} \implies 2q^* = 13 - q^* \implies q^* = \frac{13}{3}$.
4

Best response graph. Firm 1's reaction curve $q_1 = \frac{13-q_2}{2}$ is a downward-sloping line from $(0, 13/2)$ to $(13, 0)$ in $(q_2, q_1)$ space. Firm 2's curve is symmetric. The Nash equilibrium is their intersection at $(q_1^*, q_2^*) = (13/3, 13/3)$.
