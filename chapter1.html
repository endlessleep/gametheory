Static Games with Perfect Information

Modeling simultaneous strategic interactions in normal form and solving for rational equilibria.

Motivation & Foundations

In this chapter, we model and analyse static games with perfect information:

  • Static (simultaneous): All players select their strategies at the same time and independently, without observing the actions of others.
  • Perfect information: Every player knows the full structure of the game: the number of players, the complete strategy sets, and all payoff functions.

The core objective of game theory is to answer: if all players are rational, what strategies will they choose?

This is fundamentally distinct from an individual decision problem. In a decision problem, an isolated agent maximises an objective function whose parameters are exogenously given. In a strategic game, each player's payoff depends on everybody else's choices. Therefore, an optimal choice cannot be made in isolation — it depends on what you expect opponents to do, while knowing they are anticipating your choice as well.

Normal Form Games

Formal Definition
Game in Normal Form $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$

A game in normal form (or strategic form) is completely characterised by a triplet $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$:

$N$A finite set of players $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$.
$S_i$, for each $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$The strategy set (pure strategy space or action set) available to player $i$.
$g_i : \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j \to \mathbb{R}$The payoff function of player $i$, mapping every strategy profile to a real number.

Interpretation: Each player $i$ plays once, simultaneously and independently of others, by choosing an action $s_i \in S_i$. Once all actions are chosen, the strategy profile $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_N)$ is formed, and player $i$ receives the payoff $g_i(s_1, \ldots, s_N)$.

Nature of Payoffs: Payoffs are real numbers encoding players' preferences over outcomes. Only the ordinal properties matter: a higher payoff represents a strictly preferred outcome.

Decoding the Mathematical Notation

Distinguish clearly between sets of possibilities and specific selected elements:

🧠 Core Memory Rule
Capital Letter $S$ = Set
$S_i$ = Strategy set of player $i$
$S = \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j$ = Profile set of all players
$S_{-i} = \prod_{j \neq i} S_j$ = Profile set of all rivals
Lowercase Letter $s$ = Selected Action
$s_i \in S_i$ = Specific action chosen by player $i$
$s \in S$ = Specific full profile $(s_1, \ldots, s_N)$
$s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ = Actions of everyone except $i$
$S = \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j$Action set of all players (Cartesian product of individual strategy spaces).
$s = (s_1, \ldots, s_N) \in S$A strategy profile (one action specified for each player).
$s_{-i} = (s_1, \ldots, s_{i-1}, s_{i+1}, \ldots, s_N)$The action profile of all players except player $i$. Allows writing $s = (s_i, s_{-i})$.
$g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$Payoff to player $i$ when she plays $s_i$ and all opponents play $s_{-i}$.
$g = (g_1, \ldots, g_N)$The profile of all payoff functions in the game.
Strategic Interdependence: The notation $g_i(s_1, \ldots, s_N)$ shows that player $i$'s payoff is determined by everyone's simultaneous choices. Player $i$ cannot simply maximise $g_i$ alone because her objective function depends on $s_{-i}$, which is outside her direct control.

Cournot Duopoly
📋 Game Formulation Task

1 · Players

$N = \{1, 2\}$ (two rival firms producing a homogeneous product).

Total market output is $Q = q_i + q_j$.

Firm $i$'s profit function:

Example 8
Second-Price Auction (Vickrey Auction)
📋 Game Formulation Task
Formulate the Vickrey auction mechanism: define continuous strategy spaces $S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$, private valuations $v_i$, competing bid notation $m_i(b)$, tie count $M(b)$, and the piecewise payoff function $g_i(b)$.
1 · Players

$N = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ with $N \geq 2$ bidders competing for a single indivisible object.

2 · Strategy Sets

$S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$ (each bidder submits a sealed bid $b_i \geq 0$).

Parameters & Notation:

  • $v_i \geq 0$: Bidder $i$'s private valuation (willingness to pay).
  • $b_i \geq 0$: Bidder $i$'s actual submitted bid.
  • $m_i(b) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$: The highest bid among all of bidder $i$'s competitors.
  • $M(b)$: The number of bidders tied for the highest bid.
3 · Payoffs / Payoff Function

Payoff cases broken down intuitively before the formal piecewise formula:

  • Case 1 (Unique Winner, $b_i > m_i(b)$): Bidder $i$ wins and pays the second-highest bid $m_i(b)$. Her net payoff is $v_i - m_i(b)$ (value of object minus price paid).
  • Case 2 (Loser, $b_i < m_i(b)$): Bidder $i$ loses the auction, acquires nothing, and pays nothing. Her net payoff is $0$.
  • Case 3 (Tie for Highest Bid, $b_i = m_i(b)$): The tie is broken randomly; each tied bidder wins with probability $\frac{1}{M(b)}$ and pays the winning bid $m_i(b)$. Expected payoff is $\frac{v_i - m_i(b)}{M(b)}$.
$$g_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - m_i(b) & \text{if } b_i > m_i(b) \\[8pt] \dfrac{v_i - m_i(b)}{M(b)} & \text{if } b_i = m_i(b) \\[8pt] 0 & \text{if } b_i < m_i(b) \end{cases}$$
4 · Payoff Matrix

A discrete matrix is not feasible: $N \geq 2$ players with continuous action spaces $S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$. Solved analytically using dominance properties.

5 · Intuition

By paying the second-highest bid instead of your own bid, the price paid is completely separated from what you bid. Your bid determines whether you win, not what you pay.

6 · Game Classification
🔨 Mechanism Design / Sealed-Bid Auction
7 · Why is this a Game?

Bidder $i$'s probability of winning and net surplus depend directly on the highest competing bid $m_i(b)$ submitted by rivals.

Classification Overview
Strategic Families of Normal Form Games

Games are classified according to the alignment or opposition of players' interests:

Coordination Games: Common interests dominate. In pure coordination ($g_i = g_j$ everywhere, such as Location), players want to align actions without conflicting over the preferred outcome.
Pure Conflict Games: Strictly opposed interests. In zero-sum or constant-sum games ($\sum g_i = c$, such as Penalty), one player's gain is identically the other's loss. In anti-coordination games (Hawk-Dove), the optimal response to aggression is retreat.
Mixed-Motive Games: Combine coordination and conflict. Players benefit from cooperating but face competing distributional claims or incentives to defect (Battle of the Sexes, Prisoner's Dilemma, Stag Hunt, Cournot).
Section 2

Dominance in Pure Strategies

Intuition First

The first question is not yet: what will a rational player play?

It is simpler and more foundational: what would a rational player never play?

If an action $s_i$ is systematically worse than an alternative $t_i$ against every conceivable move of the opponents, no rational player will ever choose $s_i$. By eliminating such actions, we simplify the strategic problem and narrow down rational predictions.

§ 2.1

Strict Dominance

Formal Definition
Strictly Dominated Strategy

In plain English: A strategy is strictly dominated if there exists another strategy that yields a strictly higher payoff, no matter what the opponents do.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominated if $\exists\, t_i \in S_i$ such that:

$$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$$

We say that $s_i$ is strictly dominated by $t_i$, and $t_i$ strictly dominates $s_i$.

Decoding the Notation:

  • $s_i$: The candidate strategy being tested for elimination.
  • $t_i$: The alternative dominating strategy.
  • $s_{-i}$: Any strategy profile chosen by the opponents. The strict inequality ($>$) must hold for every single $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ without exception.
🔍 Practical Matrix Check for Strict Dominance

For Player 1 (Row Player): Compare rows pairwise by inspecting the first payoff in each cell. Row $s_1$ is strictly dominated by row $t_1$ if and only if, in every column, the first number in row $t_1$ is strictly greater than the first number in row $s_1$.

For Player 2 (Column Player): Compare columns pairwise by inspecting the second payoff in each cell. Column $s_2$ is strictly dominated by column $t_2$ if and only if, in every row, the second number in column $t_2$ is strictly greater than the second number in column $s_2$.

What to Remember: A rational player will never play a strictly dominated strategy. It can be safely ruled out from consideration. Strictly dominated strategies may not exist, and when they do, there may be several.
Formal Definition
Strictly Dominant Strategy

In plain English: A strategy is strictly dominant if it is strictly better than every other available strategy, no matter what the opponents do.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominant if $\forall\, t_i \in S_i \setminus \{s_i\}$:

$$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$$
Why a strictly dominant strategy is unique: Suppose both $s_i$ and $t_i$ (with $s_i \neq t_i$) were strictly dominant. Then by definition of $s_i$ being dominant, $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$. But by definition of $t_i$ being dominant, $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$. This is an immediate logical contradiction. Hence, a strictly dominant strategy is unique if it exists.

Formal Definition
Equilibrium in Strictly Dominant Strategies

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_N^*) \in S$ is an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies if for every player $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$, $s_i^*$ is a strictly dominant strategy.

$$\text{Player 1 dominant strategy } s_1^* \;+\; \text{Player 2 dominant strategy } s_2^* \;+\; \cdots \implies \text{Dominant Strategy Equilibrium } s^*$$
What to Remember: When it exists, an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies is the most robust prediction in game theory: no player needs to know or predict what rivals are doing to know their own optimal choice. It is always unique when it exists.

§ 2.2

Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)

The Chain of Strategic Reasoning

When no player possesses a strictly dominant strategy, the static game cannot be solved in a single step. However, we can reason iteratively based on common knowledge of rationality:

  1. A rational player will never play a strictly dominated strategy.
  2. All other players know this, so they deduce that this strategy will never be played.
  3. Therefore, all players can delete that strategy from their mental model, creating a smaller residual game.
  4. In this smaller game, strategies that were not previously dominated may become dominated, because the actions that formerly justified them have been eliminated.
  5. Repeat the process iteratively until no further strictly dominated strategies exist.
Formal Definition & Procedure
The IESDS Sequence $(\Gamma^k)_{k \geq 1}$

Let $\Gamma^1 = \Gamma$ be the initial game. At each iteration step $k \geq 1$:

  • If $\Gamma^k$ contains no strictly dominated strategy for any player: the procedure stops.
  • Otherwise, select a player $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$ and a strategy $s_i \in S_i^k$ that is strictly dominated in $\Gamma^k$. Construct the smaller residual game $\Gamma^{k+1}$ by setting $S_i^{k+1} = S_i^k \setminus \{s_i\}$ while keeping all other strategy sets unchanged: $S_{-i}^{k+1} = S_{-i}^k$.

Decoding the Sequence:

  • $\Gamma^1$: The full original game.
  • $\Gamma^2$: The residual game after the first strategy elimination.
  • $\Gamma^k$: The residual game at round $k$.

Solvable by IESDS: A game is said to be solvable by IESDS if the procedure terminates with exactly one strategy surviving for each player: $|S_i^k| = 1$ for all $i$. The unique surviving strategy profile is the IESDS solution.

Proposition — Order Independence of Strict IESDS

If a game is solvable by IESDS, the surviving strategy profile is completely independent of the order of elimination. Whichever strictly dominated strategy is removed first, the procedure will always converge to the exact same unique profile.

Core Takeaways:

  • An IESDS solution may not exist (the procedure may terminate with multiple strategies remaining).
  • When an IESDS solution exists, it is strictly unique.
  • IESDS relies on common knowledge of rationality at all orders: "I know that you know that I know..."

§ 2.3

Weak Dominance

Motivation & Conceptual Relaxation

In many games, no strategy is strictly dominated, and IESDS stops immediately without narrowing down the strategy space. To make progress, we relax the strict inequality ($>$) to a weak inequality ($\geq$).

A weakly dominated strategy may perform equally well as the dominating strategy against some opponent moves, but it never outperforms it and is strictly worse against at least one opponent move.

Mathematical Comparison
Strict vs. Weak Dominance Conditions
CriteriaStrict DominanceWeak Dominance
Inequality Condition $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$
Ties Permitted? No ties permitted (strictly greater everywhere). Ties permitted ($=$ allowed, but never $<$ ).
Rational Regret Certain regret: alternative $t_i$ is strictly better. Potential regret: $t_i$ is never worse, sometimes better.
$$\text{Strict Dominance: } \quad \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$$
$$\text{Weak Dominance: } \quad \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$$
Formal Definitions
Weakly Dominated and Weakly Dominant Strategies

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is weakly dominated if $\exists\, t_i \in S_i$ such that $\forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$, $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \leq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is weakly dominant if $\forall\, t_i \in S_i$, $\forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$, $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$.

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*) \in S$ is a dominant strategy equilibrium if for every player $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$, $s_i^*$ is weakly dominant.

A Crucial Methodological Warning: Unlike strict dominance, iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies (IEWDS) is order-dependent. Eliminating different weakly dominated strategies first can lead to completely different residual games and different equilibrium predictions.

Second-Price Auction: Weak Dominance of Truthful Bidding
🎯 Theorem / Analytical Proof Task
Prove that in a second-price sealed-bid auction, bidding one's true valuation ($b_i = v_i$) is a weakly dominant strategy for every bidder $i$. Fix competitors' bids $b_{-i}$ with highest competing bid $m_i(b) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$, and partition into three exhaustive cases: $v_i > m_i(b)$, $v_i < m_i(b)$, and $v_i = m_i(b)$.
1
Step 1 — Analytical Setup & Methodology:
Fix an arbitrary bidder $i$ with true valuation $v_i \geq 0$. Fix any arbitrary bid profile of opponents $b_{-i}$, and let $m_i(b) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$ be the highest competing bid.
We must show that for any alternative bid $b_i' \neq v_i$, $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$. We partition the competing bids into three mutually exclusive cases.
2
Step 2 — Case 1 ($v_i > m_i(b)$ — Truthful Bid Wins):
With truthful bid $b_i = v_i$: since $v_i > m_i(b)$, bidder $i$ wins and pays $m_i(b)$, earning payoff $g_i = v_i - m_i(b) > 0$.
Consider deviations $b_i'$:
• Overbidding ($b_i' > v_i$): Still wins and still pays $m_i(b) \implies$ payoff is $v_i - m_i(b)$ (equal).
• Underbidding ($b_i' < v_i$): If $b_i' > m_i(b)$, still wins with payoff $v_i - m_i(b)$ (equal). If $b_i' \leq m_i(b)$, she loses or ties, earning $\leq 0 < v_i - m_i(b)$ (strictly worse).
Hence, $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$.
3
Step 3 — Case 2 ($v_i < m_i(b)$ — Truthful Bid Loses):
With truthful bid $b_i = v_i$: since $v_i < m_i(b)$, bidder $i$ loses the auction, earning payoff $g_i = 0$.
Consider deviations $b_i'$:
• Underbidding ($b_i' < v_i$): Still loses, earning payoff $0$ (equal).
• Overbidding ($b_i' > v_i$): If $b_i' < m_i(b)$, still loses, earning $0$ (equal). If $b_i' > m_i(b)$, she wins the auction but pays $m_i(b) > v_i$, earning payoff $v_i - m_i(b) < 0$ (strictly worse).
Hence, $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$.
4
Step 4 — Case 3 ($v_i = m_i(b)$ — Tie with Highest Competitor):
With truthful bid $b_i = v_i = m_i(b)$: whether bidder $i$ wins or loses, price paid equals valuation, earning payoff $\frac{v_i - m_i(b)}{M(b)} = 0$.
Any deviation $b_i' < v_i$ loses with payoff $0$ (equal). Any deviation $b_i' > v_i$ wins and pays $m_i(b) = v_i$, yielding $0$ (equal).
Hence, $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$.
✓
Conclusion:
In every possible case and against all opponent bid profiles $b_{-i}$, truthful bidding yields at least as high a payoff as any alternative bid: $\forall\, b_i' \geq 0$, $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$.
Therefore, truthful bidding $b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy in the second-price auction. $\blacksquare$
Section 3

Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

§ 3.1

Definition, Best Response, and Interpretations

Intuition First

We are searching for a solution concept more general than dominance that applies to all strategic games.

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where nobody wants to change their own strategy ALONE.

The essential word is unilateral: we fix the actions of all other players ($s_{-i}$) and check whether player $i$ can achieve a strictly higher payoff by switching to an alternative action $t_i$. If no individual player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally, the profile is in balance — a Nash equilibrium.

Formal Definition
Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*) \in S$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$ and every alternative action $t_i \in S_i$:

$$\forall\; i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}, \quad \forall\; t_i \in S_i, \quad g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$$

Decoding the Mathematical Notation:

  • $s_{-i}^*$: The equilibrium choices of all opponents — held strictly fixed.
  • $t_i \in S_i$: A potential unilateral deviation by player $i$.
  • The condition requires that player $i$'s equilibrium payoff is greater than or equal to what she would get from any unilateral deviation $t_i$.
Profitable Unilateral Deviation: An action $t_i \in S_i$ is a profitable unilateral deviation from profile $s$ if $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile that has no profitable unilateral deviations for any player.
Formal Definition & Proposition
Best Response and Reformulation

In plain English: A best response is an action that maximises a player's payoff, taking the actions of all opponents as given.

Formally, $s_i \in S_i$ is a best response to $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ if $\forall\, t_i \in S_i$, $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$.

The set of all best responses of player $i$ against $s_{-i}$ is denoted $BR_i(s_{-i})$. Note: $BR_i(s_{-i})$ can contain more than one strategy when multiple actions yield the same maximal payoff.

$$s^* \in S \text{ is a Nash Equilibrium} \iff \forall\; i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}, \quad s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*)$$
Fundamental Equivalence: A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where every player is simultaneously playing a best response to all opponents. It is a mutual best response.
🔍 Practical Method — Finding Nash Equilibria in a Payoff Matrix

Step 1 — Player 1's Best Responses (Rows): For each column (each choice of Player 2), inspect the first payoff in each cell of that column. Underline the maximum value(s). These are Player 1's best responses to that column.

Step 2 — Player 2's Best Responses (Columns): For each row (each choice of Player 1), inspect the second payoff in each cell of that row. Underline the maximum value(s). These are Player 2's best responses to that row.

Step 3 — Mutual Best Responses: A cell $(s_1, s_2)$ is a Nash equilibrium if and only if both payoffs in that cell are underlined.

Epistemic Foundations
Five Interpretations of Nash Equilibrium
1. Prescription: An outside arbitrator recommends strategy profile $s^*$. If all players believe others will follow the recommendation, no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally. The recommendation is self-enforcing.
2. Pre-Play Agreement: Players communicate before the game and agree on $s^*$. Since commitments are non-binding, the agreement is only credible if it is a Nash equilibrium (no player benefits from cheating alone).
3. Introspection: Rational players analyse the game and deduce that $s^*$ is the only logically consistent outcome. (Most persuasive when the equilibrium is unique.)
4. Social Convention: Established social norms (e.g. driving on the right-hand side) persist because when everyone conforms, no individual gains by deviating alone.
5. Learning and Evolution: Through repeated play and trial-and-error adjustments, players converge to an equilibrium state where all unilateral incentives to change have vanished.

Continuous Action Game: Analytical Nash Equilibrium
🎯 Question to Solve
  1. Formulate the profit function $g_i(q_i, q_j)$ for each firm $i \in \{1, 2\}$ under inverse demand $P = \max(13 - q_1 - q_2, 0)$ and cost $c = 0$.
  2. Derive the best response function $BR_i(q_j)$ using the First-Order Condition (FOC) and verify concavity via the Second-Order Condition (SOC).
  3. Solve the system of reaction functions to find the Cournot-Nash equilibrium quantities $(q_1^*, q_2^*)$, aggregate output $Q^*$, market clearing price $P^*$, and equilibrium profits $\pi_i^*$.
1
Step 1 — Market Demand, Cost, and Profit Functions:
Inverse market demand is $P(Q) = \max(13 - q_1 - q_2, 0)$. Assuming production is non-negative and $q_1 + q_2 \leq 13$, price is $P = 13 - q_1 - q_2$.
Cost structure: constant marginal cost $c = 0$.
Firm $i$'s profit function: $$g_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i \cdot P = q_i(13 - q_i - q_j) = 13q_i - q_i^2 - q_i q_j$$
2
Step 2 — Derivation of Best Response Functions (FOC):
Firm $i$ maximises $g_i(q_i, q_j)$ over $q_i \geq 0$, treating $q_j$ as fixed.
First-order condition (FOC): $$\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q_i} = 13 - 2q_i - q_j = 0 \implies 2q_i = 13 - q_j \implies BR_i(q_j) = \max\left(\frac{13 - q_j}{2}, 0\right)$$ For Firm 1: $q_1 = BR_1(q_2) = \frac{13 - q_2}{2}$.
For Firm 2: $q_2 = BR_2(q_1) = \frac{13 - q_1}{2}$.
3
Step 3 — Second-Order Condition (SOC Verification):
Check that the critical point is indeed a global maximum: $$\frac{\partial^2 g_i}{\partial q_i^2} = -2 < 0$$ The profit function is strictly concave in $q_i$. Therefore, the best response function gives the unique optimal output with no profitable unilateral deviation.
4
Step 4 — Simultaneous Solution (Cournot-Nash Equilibrium):
A Nash equilibrium $(q_1^*, q_2^*)$ requires mutual best responses: $q_1^* = BR_1(q_2^*)$ and $q_2^* = BR_2(q_1^*)$.
Substituting $q_2^*$ into $q_1^*$: $$q_1^* = \frac{13 - \frac{13 - q_1^*}{2}}{2} = \frac{26 - 13 + q_1^*}{4} \implies 4q_1^* = 13 + q_1^* \implies 3q_1^* = 13 \implies q_1^* = \frac{13}{3}$$ By symmetry, $q_2^* = \frac{13}{3}$.
✓
Conclusion:
Cournot-Nash equilibrium quantities: $q_1^* = q_2^* = \frac{13}{3} \approx 4.33$.
Total market output: $Q^* = q_1^* + q_2^* = \frac{26}{3} \approx 8.67$.
Market clearing price: $P^* = 13 - \frac{26}{3} = \frac{13}{3} \approx 4.33$.
Equilibrium firm profits: $g_i(q_1^*, q_2^*) = \frac{13}{3} \times \frac{13}{3} = \frac{169}{9} \approx 18.78$.
Graphical interpretation: The intersection of the two downward-sloping reaction curves on the $(q_1, q_2)$ plane identifies the unique Cournot-Nash equilibrium point $(\frac{13}{3}, \frac{13}{3})$.

Proposition 1 — Strict Dominance, IESDS, and Nash Equilibria

(1) An equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.

(2) Let $(\Gamma^k)_{k \geq 1}$ be any IESDS sequence under strict dominance. Then for all $k \geq 1$: $$NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$$

(3) If a game $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS, the resulting strategy profile is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.

🎯 Mathematical Proof Task
Provide a rigorous mathematical proof showing that:
  1. An equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium, and it is strictly unique.
  2. If a game is solvable by IESDS, the resulting strategy profile is the unique Nash equilibrium.
1
Step 1 — Proof of Statement (1) (Existence):
Let $s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ be an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies.
By definition, for every player $i$ and all $t_i \neq s_i^*$, $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$.
Evaluating this inequality at the specific profile $s_{-i} = s_{-i}^*$ gives $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $t_i \neq s_i^*$.
Therefore, $s^*$ satisfies the Nash equilibrium condition.
2
Step 2 — Proof of Statement (1) (Uniqueness):
Suppose there exists another Nash equilibrium $s' \neq s^*$. Then there exists at least one player $i$ such that $s_i' \neq s_i^*$.
Because $s_i^*$ is strictly dominant, $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}') > g_i(s_i', s_{-i}')$.
This strictly contradicts the assumption that $s'$ is a Nash equilibrium (which requires $s_i'$ to be a best response against $s_{-i}'$).
Hence, no other Nash equilibrium can exist. $s^*$ is strictly unique.
3
Step 3 — Proof of Statement (3) (IESDS Convergence):
By Statement (2), eliminating strictly dominated strategies never adds or removes any Nash equilibrium: $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$ for every round $k$.
When $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS, the terminal game $\Gamma^k$ consists of a single strategy profile $\{\bar{s}\}$.
In a single-profile game, that unique profile is trivially the only Nash equilibrium: $NE(\Gamma^k) = \{\bar{s}\}$.
Since $NE(\Gamma) = NE(\Gamma^k)$, we conclude that $NE(\Gamma) = \{\bar{s}\}$.
✓
Conclusion:
Statements (1), (2), and (3) are proven. $\blacksquare$
Proposition 2 — Weak Dominance and Nash Equilibria

(1) An equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium (though not necessarily unique).

(2) Under iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies: $$NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$$ The inclusion may be strict: weakly dominated strategies can themselves be part of a Nash equilibrium, and eliminating them removes those equilibria from the game.

🎯 Mathematical Proof Task
  1. Prove that an equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies is necessarily a Nash equilibrium.
  2. Provide a counter-example proving that eliminating weakly dominated strategies can eliminate Nash equilibria ($NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subset NE(\Gamma^k)$).
1
Step 1 — Proof of Statement (1):
Let $s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ be an equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies.
By definition, for every player $i$ and all $t_i \in S_i$, $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$.
Evaluating at $s_{-i} = s_{-i}^*$ yields $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $t_i \in S_i$.
This is identically the definition of a Nash equilibrium.
2
Step 2 — Counter-Example Proving Strict Inclusion in Statement (2):
Consider the matrix game with Player 1 rows $\{U, D\}$ and Player 2 columns $\{L, R\}$ with payoffs: $(U,L)=(1,1)$, $(U,R)=(1,1)$, $(D,L)=(0,0)$, $(D,R)=(1,1)$.
Both $(U,L)$ and $(D,R)$ are Nash equilibria.
However, $D$ is weakly dominated by $U$ for Player 1 ($1 \geq 0$ and $1 \geq 1$). Eliminating $D$ creates a residual game where $(D,R)$ no longer exists.
Hence, eliminating weakly dominated strategies destroys the Nash equilibrium $(D,R)$, proving that $NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subset NE(\Gamma^k)$ can be a strict subset.
✓
Conclusion:
Statements (1) and (2) are proven. $\blacksquare$

1
Step 1: Check for Strictly Dominant Strategies

For each player $i$, test if one strategy yields strictly higher payoffs than all alternatives against every opponent profile. If every player has a strictly dominant strategy, the resulting profile $s^*$ is the unique dominant strategy equilibrium and the unique Nash equilibrium. Stop.

2
Step 2: Run Strict IESDS (Iterated Elimination)

Iteratively eliminate all strategies that are strictly dominated in the current residual game $\Gamma^k$. If the process terminates with exactly one strategy per player, the game is solvable by IESDS and this profile is the unique Nash equilibrium. (Strict IESDS never eliminates Nash equilibria). Stop.

3
Step 3: Find Nash Equilibria via Best Responses

If multiple strategies survive IESDS (or no strategies were dominated), compute best responses:
• Matrix games: For each column underline Player 1's highest first payoff; for each row underline Player 2's highest second payoff. Cells with both payoffs underlined are the Nash equilibria.
• Continuous games: Differentiate profit functions to find reaction functions $BR_i(s_{-i})$ (FOCs), verify concavity (SOCs), and solve simultaneously.
